Cancer data is buried in the attachments.

Before 2023, the normal way to share a cancer dataset was to attach a spreadsheet to the paper. Those files still exist, but no catalogue lists them. We counted every one across 30,376 NCI-funded open-access papers, opened 300 papers' worth by hand, and packaged the first rescues.

How we counted

Two independent methods, and they agree.

  1. 01Census. Europe PMC's search finds every NCI-funded open-access paper from 2016 to 2022 that has supplementary files and cites no accession in GEO, SRA, dbGaP, EGA, ArrayExpress, PRIDE, BioProject, MetaboLights or PDB. We read each paper's full text and listed its attachments by filename, size and caption. 89,820 attachments in 28,949 readable papers. Nothing downloaded.
  2. 02Sample. 300 random glioblastoma papers from that population. Every attachment downloaded, every spreadsheet, CSV, Word and PDF file opened. A file counts as a dataset if its largest table has at least 50 rows and 3 columns, or at least 1,000 rows. Peer-review letters, reporting summaries and figures are excluded automatically.
  3. 03Hand check. A person opened 45 of the machine's decisions. All 30 files it called datasets were real data tables; 14 of the 15 it rejected were correctly rejected.
  4. 04Package. Eight datasets from CC BY papers were packaged unmodified with DataCite-style metadata, ready for deposit in an NIH-recognized repository. Nothing has been uploaded yet.

What this does not claim

  • The census counts files by name. The 20% "genuine dataset" rate comes from opening files in one cancer type; three more cancer types are being measured now.
  • About half of the rescued tables are analysis results (gene lists, pathway scores) rather than raw measurements. Both are reusable; each record says which it is.
  • Roughly one in five census papers, mostly PLOS and BMC-family journals, already has a publisher-assigned Figshare identifier for its attachments. Those files still lack a dataset description and a catalogue entry.
  • About 5% of papers could not be read due to server errors and are excluded. Zip archives (3,203) were not opened. The counts are slight undercounts.
  • Large-scale extraction of supplementary files was done before us (Gobeill et al., 2025, 36 million files). The new step is classifying, describing, depositing and cataloguing them for the funder's own index.
