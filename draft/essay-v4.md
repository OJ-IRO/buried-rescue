# Attachment Rescue: giving NCI's buried supplementary datasets a permanent home

**Track 1 (Ideas). Draft v4, 2026-09-23. Not submitted.** Every number traces to `pilot/PILOT_REPORT.md` and `pilot/data/census_summary_v3.json`.

---

## Prompt 1: Significance and Approach

**The gap.** Before the 2023 NIH Data Management and Sharing Policy, the normal way to share a cancer dataset was to attach it to the paper: gene expression tables, patient cohort tables, drug screening results and mass spectrometry measurements, saved as Excel files and uploaded as "Supplementary Data 3." Those files still exist in PubMed Central. But no dataset catalogue lists them, no search indexes their contents, and nothing describes what is inside. A researcher looking for glioblastoma cohort data with survival and IDH status cannot find a 121-patient table that has sat in a supplementary file since 2018 without already knowing the paper.

**How big it is.** We measured it. Europe PMC lists 58,469 NCI-funded open-access papers from 2016 to 2022; 26,228 have supplementary files and no reference, as detected by Europe PMC's text mining, to any of ten data repositories (GEO, SRA, dbGaP, EGA, ArrayExpress, PRIDE, BioProject, MetaboLights, PDB, GenBank). We drew random samples of 300 papers for each of four cancers (glioblastoma, breast, colorectal, leukemia), downloaded every attachment, and opened every spreadsheet, CSV, Word and PDF file. A file counts as a dataset only if its largest table has at least 50 rows and 3 columns, or 1,000 rows. **The four cancers agree within three points: pooled, 202 of 1,076 papers (19%) hold a data table and 156 (14.5%, confidence interval 12% to 17%) hold it as a native spreadsheet rather than a table inside a PDF.** 167 of the 202 (83%) are CC BY or CC0, so their files can be re-deposited today. Tables ran up to 152,683 rows. The classifier's decisions were re-examined file by file on 90 files (with the AI assistant, not blind; verdicts published): all 60 it called datasets were real data tables, and every table it missed was under 50 rows. A blind human check is next. We then ran a census of the full population by reading each paper's full text for its attachment list, downloading nothing. Across 25,138 readable papers there are 73,795 attachments. **4,759 papers (18.9%) carry at least one spreadsheet, CSV or tab-delimited text attachment, 15,458 such files in total, 15.9 gigabytes of Excel alone.** 3,824 of those papers (80%) are licensed CC BY or CC0. After discounting papers that cite a repository somewhere our filter missed (15%, checked on 500 papers) and files already mirrored on Figshare (13.5%, verified file by file), the estimate is roughly 2,800 to 3,900 papers holding 8,000 to 11,000 buried dataset files, about three-quarters under a license that permits re-deposit. The authors say so themselves: in the same 500 papers, 17% of data-availability statements say the data is "in the paper or supplementary files" and 15% say "available on request." {{POST2023}}

**Who is affected.** Researchers, because data they need is unfindable; trainees and small labs most of all, because they lack the network that knows which paper to open. Students, patient advocates and Data Jamboree participants, told computing skills are not required, who then meet a literature that hides its data in attachments. The patients: verified patient-level tables in our four samples alone describe about 4,400 patient records, roughly 500 from institutional cohorts we could not find in TCGA, GEO, dbGaP or any catalogue. The original authors, whose data earns no citations because it has no identifier. And NCI, which paid for these datasets twice and receives no reuse in return.

**Why it matters to NCI and ODS goals.** These papers were compliant. They followed the norms of their time, and the data still vanished from view: the situation the challenge describes when it says policy alone does not drive culture change. It also bears on NCI's commitment to broad, rapid and equitable sharing: data only insiders can find is not broadly shared. ODS's own workshop report names "incomplete, incorrect, or missing metadata" and the findability of siloed data as major bottlenecks; its Data Jamboree paper names "limited awareness of available datasets" as a primary barrier. A supplementary file is the purest case of both: real data with no metadata, in a silo of one.

**The approach.** A retrospective rescue, run over public infrastructure that already exists:

1. **Find.** Query Europe PMC for NCI-funded open-access papers with supplementary files and no repository accession; read each paper's full text to list its attachments. One call per paper, no downloads.
2. **Sort.** Download the tabular attachments and classify each: dataset, small table, or not data. Separate primary measurements (cohort tables, expression tables, counts, spectra) from derived results (gene lists, pathway tables). Skip peer-review files, reporting summaries and figures.
3. **Describe.** Generate a standard dataset record for each, in the DataCite format that repositories and search engines read: title, creators and license from the article, article DOI and PMC identifier, funder, format, size, sheet names, and a one-sentence plain-language description of what the table contains. A person spot-checks.
4. **Deposit or register.** For CC BY and CC0 articles, deposit the unmodified file in one of the free general-purpose repositories NIH already recognizes, which assigns it a permanent identifier (a DOI). For no-derivatives licenses, and for any file whose authors object, register a metadata record pointing to the file's existing location in PubMed Central instead of copying it. Who deposits, and how authors are told, is addressed below.
5. **List.** Deliver the rescued records to the Index of NCI Studies as a curated source file in the format its pipeline already accepts. We have produced that file for the 462 rescued datasets, in the index's exact 27-column schema; adding them is one load, not new infrastructure.
6. **Keep alive.** Check every rescued link and identifier monthly and publish the result.

Steps 1 through 3 have run on 1,200 papers across four cancers, and step 4 as far as packaging: eight glioblastoma datasets sit ready for deposit with complete metadata, awaiting the governance decision below. We also put three rescued glioma tables back to work. Two TCGA-derived tables from different papers join cleanly on 244 shared patients and, pooled to 1,037, reproduce the known IDH survival split; the third is a 121-patient hospital cohort found in no catalogue. The proposal is to run the whole pipeline over the full NCI corpus, cancer by cancer.

## Prompt 2: Potential Impact on the Cancer Research Community

**Immediate.** Thousands of existing cancer datasets become findable, citable and described: by the rates above, 8,000 to 11,000 buried datasets in the NCI corpus alone, about three-quarters freely re-depositable. None of this requires new data; the cost of finding them is all that stands between them and reuse.

**Breadth.** The gap is not specific to any cancer or to cancer itself. We ran the same filename census on 300-paper samples from five other institutes: NHLBI 15%, NIDDK 18%, NIMH 19%, NIA 21%, NINDS 24% of papers carry a spreadsheet attachment, against 18% for NCI. Those five institutes add 63,000 papers to the population. Because the pipeline is defined by a funder tag and a date range, any NCI Division, Office or Center, or any NIH institute, can run it unchanged.

**For non-data scientists.** A rescued dataset arrives with a plain-language description and a stable identifier. A community college instructor can hand students a real patient cohort table with a DOI. A Data Jamboree team can search the catalogue for "cohort with survival data" and get a hit. We propose offering the rescued collection to the November 2026 NCI Data Jamboree, putting rescued data in front of exactly the users the challenge names, at an event ODS runs.

**For authors and for credit.** Each rescued dataset gains an identifier linked to its article, so invisible data can start to accrue citations. Papers whose data statements link to a repository receive up to 25% more citations (Colavizza et al., 2020); correlational, but rescue gives authors that identifier at no effort to them.

**For policy and infrastructure across NCI.** The census gives ODS something it does not currently have: a per-year, per-journal account of how much NCI-funded data was shared only as attachments and how much is legally recoverable (Nature Communications alone: 889 papers, 3,201 spreadsheet files). {{MECH}} That informs data-sharing guidance, lets program officers report the public benefit of past investments concretely, and gives the Index of NCI Studies its first source of datasets drawn from publications.

## Prompt 3: Innovation and Awareness of Existing Efforts

**What exists.** 
- **Large-scale extraction of supplementary files has been done.** Gobeill and colleagues (Bioinformatics Advances, 2025) processed 36.2 million supplementary files from 6.2 million PubMed Central articles, found that 97% of database accession numbers appear only in supplements, and built a text search index; the Europe PMC team (Kafkas et al., 2015) mined supplements earlier. Both treat supplements as text to search, not datasets to catalogue; neither classifies files as data, produces dataset metadata, assigns identifiers, or lists anything in a discovery catalogue.
- **The files are already mirrored, twice, as files.** EMBL-EBI's BioStudies holds one record per Europe PMC article (for example S-EPMC6193287) listing its supplementary files under the article title and abstract; PLOS assigns component DOIs to supporting files, and Springer Nature and BMC mirror theirs to Figshare. All of these are per-article file dumps: no record says which file is a dataset, what it contains, or who is in it, none carries a dataset identifier, and none reaches NCI's index. Mirroring is the storage half; rescue is the description and catalogue half. We checked our census against Figshare: files from BMC and other Springer Nature journals, 13.5% of census spreadsheet papers when verified file by file, already have a Figshare identifier; files from Nature Communications, Scientific Reports, eLife, Oncotarget and Cell Press journals, the large majority, do not. Even the mirrored files have no dataset description and do not reach NCI's index, so they need the sorting, describing and listing steps but not the deposit step.
- **The scale of the problem is documented.** Federer et al. (2018) found 69% of PLOS ONE data availability statements pointed to the paper or its supplements and only 18% to a repository; Colavizza et al. (2020) found repository links in 21% of PLOS and 12% of BMC papers. Federer (2022) found DOIs stay resolvable far more often than plain links, which is why rescued files get one.
- **The catalogue side exists.** The Index of NCI Studies, managed by ODS and built by CBIIT, ingests datasets from GEO, SRA, dbGaP and several curated NCI sources through a public pipeline, but nothing from general-purpose repositories or publications. The INS team already runs automated enrichment over its records, including a published tool that maps study text to standard disease codes, so a curated source of publication-derived datasets is a next step for a pipeline built to take curated sources, not a new capability. NIH's Generalist Repository Ecosystem Initiative, which coordinates seven general-purpose repositories, has no retrospective harvest component.

**What is new.** The combination, and the direction. We found no effort that has taken a funder's back catalogue, classified which attachments are datasets, described them, given them identifiers, and delivered them into the funder's own catalogue. Extraction exists; deposit and listing do not. Mirroring is prospective; rescue is retrospective. Every component is standard; what is new is pointing them backwards at data shared in good faith and lost anyway.

**What we are not proposing.** Not a new repository, catalogue or metadata standard, and not a replacement for the 2023 policy. Rescue is the backfill for everything published before it.

## Prompt 4: Transferability, Sustainability, and Feasibility

**Path to adoption.** Every component is public and already operated by someone else: Europe PMC serves the papers and attachments, general-purpose repositories assign identifiers for free, and the Index of NCI Studies pipeline is open source and ingests curated source files. Adoption means one new curated source in that pipeline, a decision inside NCI rather than new infrastructure.

**What it would take.** Seconds of compute and cents of storage per paper. The census needs no downloads; rescuing a cancer the size of glioblastoma is a few hundred files. Human time goes to spot-checking metadata and to one conversation with the INS team about the source file format, which is already published.

**Constraints, stated plainly.**

- *Licensing.* 80% of the spreadsheet-bearing papers are CC BY or CC0 and their files can be deposited unchanged. About 9% are CC BY-NC-ND, a license that forbids modified copies; those get a metadata record pointing at PubMed Central rather than a copy. Author manuscripts outside the open-access subset are excluded. The pipeline enforces this by license tag before anything is copied.
- *Who deposits, and author consent.* We do not propose that a third party silently uploads authors' files. Two options, in order of preference: NCI or ODS acts as depositor under an institutional repository account, as NIH already does for NIH-authored manuscripts in PubMed Central; or authors are notified 60 days before deposit with a one-click opt-out, and files whose authors object receive a metadata-only record. Either way the record names the original authors as creators, links the article, carries a route for correction or removal, and NCI never owns the data. Tables describing human participants, even de-identified, get a review step before any copy is made.
- *Signal versus noise.* Most attachments are figures and methods; about two-thirds of sampled papers had no tabular data at all. The classifier keeps that noise out, and its accuracy is published. About half of rescued tables are derived results rather than raw measurements, and each record says which.
- *Duplication.* Some attachments duplicate data deposited elsewhere, and some already carry a publisher-assigned Figshare identifier. Records are matched against existing repository entries by article DOI and filename before deposit, and an existing identifier is reused, not duplicated.
- *The INS intake.* The one dependency on others' decision. If INS does not add the source, the datasets still exist with DOIs in repositories NIH already recognizes, and the census still stands.

**Why it persists.** After the backlog, the pipeline runs quarterly on new open-access papers and monthly on link health, both as scheduled checks. What outlives any team is the curated source file and the census, both public and reproducible.
